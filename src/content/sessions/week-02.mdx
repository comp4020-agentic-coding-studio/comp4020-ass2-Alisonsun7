## Core question

> Is the larger or cheaper-looking package actually better value?

Week 1 showed that a single price hides several different numbers. This week
does the same trick with quantity. Supermarkets already compute **unit
price** for you — the small print under the tag, price divided by weight or
volume — because regulators decided shoppers couldn't be trusted to do the
division themselves. They were right that the division is annoying. They
were not right that doing it is the whole story.

## Usable quantity

Unit price answers "which pack is cheaper per unit". It does not answer
"which pack is cheaper per unit **I will use**". A 2 kg bag of spinach at a
lower unit price than a 200 g bag is only a better deal if most of that 2 kg
gets eaten before it liquefies in the crisper drawer. The relevant
denominator is not the quantity in the pack; it is the quantity that
converts into something you wanted.

## Bulk purchasing and shelf life

Bulk discounts are real, but they trade a price advantage for a waste risk,
and waste risk is not printed on the shelf label. A rational bulk purchase
requires an honest estimate of how much you'll use before it spoils, expires,
or you simply lose interest — an estimate the unit-price sticker cannot make
for you.

> **Lowest unit price is only useful when the quantity is actually used.**

## The $249 headphones

The headphones don't have a unit-price problem in the literal sense — you
can't buy 70% of a pair of headphones — but the same logic applies to
bundles. A $249 pair sold alone and a $279 "bundle" with a carry case and a
spare cable are only comparable once you price the case and cable at what
they're actually worth to you, not at whatever number makes the bundle look
generous.

## Learning outcomes

By the end of this week, you should be able to:

- define unit price as total price divided by quantity
- distinguish "quantity in the pack" from "quantity you will actually use"
- compute both readings for a two-pack comparison using the calculator above
- explain why a bulk discount can still be the worse purchase
- connect this week's usable-quantity idea to a bundle price, such as
  headphones sold with accessories

## Before class

Bring the price and size of two competing pack sizes of something you
actually buy — two coffee bags, two boxes of the same cereal, two bottle
sizes of the same shampoo. Real numbers, not estimates.

## In class

Using the pack sizes you brought, compute the unit price for both packs in
the calculator, then estimate honestly how much of the larger pack you'd
actually use before it spoiled, expired, or you lost interest. Does the
"cheaper per unit" pack survive that second calculation?

## What you should leave knowing

Lowest price per unit only helps if the units get used; the honest usage
estimate, not the shelf sticker, is what turns a unit price into an actual
saving.
