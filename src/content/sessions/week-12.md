## Core question

> What should a rational buying decision actually optimise?

Eleven weeks have added one complication at a time to a single number: what
"$249" means (Week 1), what quantity actually gets used (Week 2), what the
discount was measured against (Week 3), how rules combine (Week 4), what the
number has done over time (Week 5), whether it depends on who's asking
(Week 6), what it costs to keep paying (Week 7), what a reward point is
really worth (Week 8), whether "new, from this store" was even the right
category (Week 9), whether waiting is a good bet (Week 10), and what your
own time is worth while making that bet (Week 11).

None of those weeks said "ignore the price". Each said "the price is not
the only number in this decision, and sometimes it isn't even the most
important one."

## The synthesis

A complete purchase decision weighs, at minimum:

- **price** — after real discounts, not badge discounts (Weeks 1, 3, 4)
- **quantity and waste** — what actually gets used (Week 2)
- **history and context** — whether this price is typical for this buyer
  (Weeks 5, 6)
- **ongoing cost** — subscriptions, points, and their real value (Weeks 7, 8)
- **alternatives** — used, repaired, rented, or not this category at all
  (Week 9)
- **uncertainty and time** — the expected value of waiting, minus what
  waiting costs to do (Weeks 10, 11)
- **personal priorities** — values a calculator can surface but cannot
  supply, like risk tolerance, environmental preference, or simply wanting
  the thing now

## The conclusion is not "find the lowest number"

If the semester has a single takeaway, it is not:

> Find the lowest price.

It's closer to:

> A good purchase is not necessarily the one with the lowest sticker price.
> It is the best decision given the information, constraints, and values
> that actually matter.

Sometimes that decision is the cheapest option. Sometimes it's paying full
price for the option that arrives today, from a seller you trust, with no
30-day gamble attached — and that is not a failure to optimise. It's what
optimising the right thing actually looks like once "the right thing" stops
meaning "the smallest number".

## The $249 headphones, decided

Every earlier fact about the headphones is still on the table: the $349
reference price, the bundle, the sale history, the loyalty points, the $140
used alternative, the possible future sale, the hours already spent
searching (and correctly ignored). None of it produces a single universally
correct answer — that was never the goal. What it produces is a buyer who
can say exactly why they bought what they bought, at the price they paid,
and defend every part of that reasoning.

## Where this goes

Assessment 3, **Build a Buying Engine**, asks for exactly this: a system
that takes in the semester's inputs and produces a recommendation it can
explain, not one that simply outputs the lowest number.
