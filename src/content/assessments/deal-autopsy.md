## The brief

> Find one real promotion that looks like a good deal, and show exactly why
> it is — or isn't.

Pick a real advertised promotion (a screenshot or link is fine as evidence).
Identify:

- the advertised claim ("50% off", "buy one get one half price", etc.)
- the reference price it's measured against, and how you'd verify it
- anything missing from the headline: exclusions, minimum spend, expiry,
  shipping
- the actual effective saving, computed, not eyeballed
- which framing techniques from Week 3 are doing work on the reader

Then reach a judgement: is this a genuinely good price, a mediocre one
dressed up, or something in between — and say exactly what evidence gets you
there. "Marketing is misleading" is not a conclusion this brief accepts on
its own; you have to show the arithmetic that makes it true or false for
this specific promotion.

## Evidence packet

Your chosen promotion should be specific enough that another student could
reconstruct your reasoning. Include:

- a screenshot or link showing the advertised claim
- the date you captured it
- the displayed sale price and displayed reference price, if one is present
- any visible restrictions: expiry, categories, exclusions, member-only
  pricing, shipping, minimum spend, or stock limits
- any assumption you had to make because the retailer did not provide enough
  information

The promotion may be boring. In fact, a boring example with clean evidence is
better than a spectacular badge whose source disappears before it can be
checked.

## Autopsy template

Use this structure unless you have a stronger one:

1. **Claim:** what the advertisement wants the shopper to believe.
2. **Baseline:** what price the claim is measured against, and whether that
   baseline is recent, rarely-real, unverifiable, or missing.
3. **Restrictions:** what conditions change the apparent saving.
4. **Calculation:** the effective saving in dollars, including fees or
   threshold spending where relevant.
5. **Judgement:** whether the promotion is good, weak, or undecidable from
   the available evidence.

## What you submit

A short written autopsy (roughly 500–800 words, or equivalent in a
structured format) covering the five points above, with the promotion's
source cited or screenshotted. Any invented numbers used to fill a gap in
public information must be clearly labelled as an assumption, not presented
as the retailer's own figure.

## What strong work looks like

Strong work separates what is known from what is inferred. It does not simply
say "50% off is misleading"; it shows which denominator the 50% used, whether
that denominator is trustworthy, and how much money a buyer would actually
save under the stated restrictions. The best autopsies also notice when the
available evidence is insufficient and say so clearly rather than forcing a
larger conclusion.

## Common traps

- treating the printed reference price as true without checking it
- reporting the advertised percentage instead of computing dollar saving
- ignoring shipping, membership or minimum-spend conditions
- using invented assumptions without labelling them as assumptions
- reaching a moral judgement before the arithmetic has done any work
